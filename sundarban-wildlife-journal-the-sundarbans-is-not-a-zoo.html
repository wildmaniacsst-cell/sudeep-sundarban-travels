The Sundarbans Is Not a Zoo: A Real Encounter That Reveals How Tourists Miss Wildlife

Some visitors come hoping to see a tiger. Others arrive for the food, drinks, music and a good time. Sometimes, both groups share the same river—and have completely different ideas about what they are experiencing.

By Sumit Ghosh · Sudeep Sundarban Travels · Field memory: 12 March 2022
Look closely. The tigress was so well hidden in the mangrove vegetation that a casual glance from the river could easily miss her. The photograph was made using a DSLR camera and telephoto zoom lens.

“Where are the tigers? There aren't any where they took us!”

A few days ago, I remembered a conversation I had overheard at a roadside tea stall along the southern bypass. Some tourists had just returned from the Sundarbans.

One of them sounded thoroughly disappointed. “Bloody hell, there wasn't a single tiger where they took us! You have to go inside the core area if you actually want a chance.”

Another man confidently replied, “This won't work like this. My uncle's friend is a forest officer. I'll get hold of him one day. He'll take us straight to a tiger's den. Just wait and see!”

A third gentleman, apparently far less concerned about wildlife sightings, had a completely different opinion.

“I saw a tiger at the Jharkhali zoo. What difference does it make? I have no regrets. The food was absolutely brilliant, though. That fish fry with drinks in the morning was fantastic!”

The first man immediately joined in. “Forget the fish fry. That prawn malai curry at lunch was incredible. Montu's caterer really knows how to arrange things.”

The second man had a complaint of his own. “Montu is charging too much. My friends went with a group from their neighbourhood. Three thousand per head! But bloody hell, they fed them like kings. Tiger prawns, mutton, chilli chicken, biryani, everything! Though, to be fair, they were forty-five people.”

The conversation was now firmly focused on food. Then someone spotted another member of their group approaching.

“Here comes Laltu, the absolute menace! At Dobanki Watchtower, he was shouting at the girls on the boat across the river, trying to call a tiger. ‘O mama-mama! Come out! Look, your beautiful aunties have come to meet you!’”

The tea stall erupted in laughter. Someone else added, “And Ratan-da was dancing with the Jhumur performers after a few drinks last night!” Another voice chimed in, “When Raja was singing on the boat, Ratan-da nearly danced himself over the railing and into the river!”

Everyone laughed. And that, unfortunately, is not an entirely unfamiliar picture of Sundarban tourism.

The Sundarbans: A Forest or a Floating Picnic?

For a considerable number of tourists, a Sundarban trip is primarily an opportunity to eat, drink, sing, dance and have a good time with friends or family on a boat.

There is absolutely nothing wrong with enjoying a holiday. People work hard, and they deserve to relax.

But when the Sundarbans becomes little more than a floating picnic venue, the forest itself often becomes a background decoration.

The irony is that some visitors may pass close to wildlife without even realising it. Their attention is occupied by loud conversations, music, food and entertainment. Even when an animal is somewhere nearby, they may not notice subtle signs of its presence.

And occasionally, they do see a tiger. Then the excitement takes over. Shouting, screaming, calling the animal, waving and making noise can turn a quiet wildlife encounter into a chaotic spectacle in seconds.

The Tigress We Had Been Looking For

The photograph accompanying this story captures one such moment from the Sundarbans, dated 12 March 2022.

We had been following a possible route and searching for this tigress. Eventually, we located her and managed to photograph her. She was sitting deep within dense mangrove foliage—so effectively concealed that, without carefully scanning the vegetation, a visitor could easily pass the same stretch of river without ever noticing her.

I photographed her using my DSLR camera and telephoto zoom lens. The camera revealed what was extremely difficult to distinguish with the naked eye from our position.

She was about to move towards the river. At that moment, another tourist boat approached, carrying a group that was thoroughly enjoying its own world of food, conversation and celebration.

We moved our boat back and positioned ourselves in the middle of the river, keeping distance. The tigress noticed the approaching boat and slipped behind the cover of vegetation.

The other boat continued forward, making plenty of noise, passing between us and the tigress. And just like that, the moment changed. She disappeared into cover. The tourists continued their party, apparently unaware of what had just happened around them.

“You Didn't Even Tell Them There Was a Tiger?”

Someone might reasonably accuse us of being selfish. “There was a tiger right there! Why didn't you tell the people on the other boat?”

Fair question.

But imagine what would have happened. “O MAMA! MAMA! YOUR AUNTIES ARE HERE!” The shouting could have started before anyone properly understood where the animal was. The tigress would have had every reason to retreat further into cover, and we might have lost the opportunity to observe her quietly.

So, yes, we chose not to announce her presence. Sometimes, the best thing you can do when you encounter wildlife is simply to remain quiet.

Not every tiger needs an audience. Not every sighting needs to become a shouting match. And not every wildlife encounter needs to be shared with an entire river at full volume.

In the Sundarbans, Keep Your Eyes and Ears Open

The Sundarbans is not a zoo where animals appear on demand. It is a living tidal wilderness. Wildlife moves according to its own needs and circumstances. A tiger may appear unexpectedly, remain hidden for hours, or never be seen during an entire trip.

A wildlife expedition requires patience, observation and a willingness to appreciate the forest beyond its most famous resident.

Look at the banks. Watch the mudflats. Pay attention to birds, movement in the vegetation, changes in the river and the sounds of the forest. Sometimes the tiger is not the only thing worth observing.

And sometimes, if you spend the entire trip eating, drinking, singing and shouting at the forest to produce a tiger, you may miss the very thing you came to see.

The Sundarbans rewards observation, not noise.

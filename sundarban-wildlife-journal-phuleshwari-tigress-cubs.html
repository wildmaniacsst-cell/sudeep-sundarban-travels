Phuleshwari and Her Battle of Motherhood

An unforgettable Sundarbans encounter that reminded us when to observe, and when to walk away.

By Sumit Ghosh · 12 years in the Sundarbans

A famous quote attributed to Agatha Christie says that a mother's love knows no boundaries and dares everything to protect her child. After spending 12 years exploring the mangrove forests of the Sundarbans, I never imagined I would witness such a powerful demonstration of maternal instinct in the wild.

Due to health reasons, I had been away from the forest for several months. On January 23, 2026, I finally returned at the request of my team. Although our expeditions continue throughout the season, I am not always physically present in the Sundarbans. My wonderful team has been managing our operations with dedication, ensuring that every guest continues to experience the forest with care.

It was a particularly special day. It was Netaji's birthday, Saraswati Puja, and my first day back in the forest that season. I was accompanied by 12 members of a wildlife group from Mumbai.

Around 11:00 AM, as our boat entered the creeks, we received information about a male tiger. We spent the next few hours searching, but there was no sign of him. Little did we know that the forest had something far more extraordinary waiting for us.

Soon, news arrived that a tigress with two cubs had been spotted moving towards another canal.

The Queen of the Sundarbans

At approximately 1:30 PM, an unforgettable natural history drama began unfolding before us.

Standing before us was Phuleshwari, a magnificent tigress, accompanied by her two cubs, barely five months old.

For such young cubs, crossing a tidal creek is no easy task. The salty water, unfamiliar surroundings, several tourist boats, and the noise of engines appeared to make them increasingly nervous.

Phuleshwari, however, seemed determined.

She attempted to guide her cubs across the creek three times. Each time, she swam confidently towards the middle of the water, then turned back to check whether her little ones were following. The cubs tried. They entered the water and swam halfway, but fear eventually overwhelmed them. Each time, they turned back towards the bank.

Their mother returned to them again and again. Watching her movements, it almost felt as though she was reassuring them: Don't be afraid. I am here.

It was a remarkable display of patience, persistence, and maternal instinct.

A Mother's Call Across the Creek

By around 2:30 PM, the situation became even more dramatic.

Phuleshwari crossed the creek alone, perhaps hoping that seeing their mother on the opposite bank would encourage the cubs to follow. But the little ones remained behind. For a moment, the mother and her cubs were separated by the water.

Then came a sound I had never experienced in my 12 years in the Sundarbans.

The tigress began calling to her cubs. It was a deep, haunting sound, carrying an unmistakable sense of urgency. She repeatedly looked towards the bushes, searching for them, calling again and waiting.

It was difficult to witness. Two guests in our group became emotional. One of them turned towards me and said:

“Sumit Da, if we stay here, they may never be able to cross. As mothers, we cannot bear to watch this. Let's leave.”

Those words made us reflect on what was happening around us. Our presence, along with the presence of numerous boats, could be adding to the cubs' fear. Our desire to witness this rare wildlife behaviour was becoming more important than the animals' need for space.

And that was something we needed to change.

When We Chose to Leave

After waiting for approximately 15 minutes and seeing that the cubs were still reluctant to cross, Phuleshwari returned to them.

At that moment, the boats in the area made a collective decision to move away from the location and allow the family some privacy.

We left.

No photograph, no sighting, and no wildlife experience should come at the cost of unnecessarily disturbing an animal, especially when it is trying to protect its young.

Sometimes, the most meaningful wildlife encounter is not the one where we get the perfect photograph. It is the one where we recognise when our presence is no longer welcome.

A Lesson the Forest Taught Us

In 12 years of exploring the Sundarbans, I had never witnessed such a dramatic and emotional scene. Even experienced guides, some with more than 23 years in the forest, told us they had never seen this particular behaviour unfold quite like this.

Phuleshwari reminded us that the wild is not merely a place to search for sightings. It is a world of relationships, instincts, fear, courage, struggle, and survival.

That afternoon, we witnessed something that felt deeply familiar to us as humans, yet entirely wild and natural in its own way: a mother trying everything she could to help her young ones overcome their fear.

The forest gave us a rare privilege. And our responsibility was to know when to step back.

After all, the Sundarbans does not exist for our entertainment. We are visitors in the lives of its wild inhabitants.

Responsible wildlife observation: When an animal's behaviour suggests stress or when our presence may interfere with natural activity, creating distance and leaving the scene can be the most responsible choice.

On January 24, the very next day, another unforgettable encounter awaited us: the sighting of a massive male tiger. That story is coming next.

Until then, let the forest remain wild.

Sumit Ghosh
